# Eleven style decision recipes

These recipes turn the shipped visual grammar into choices for new content. They complement [STYLES.md](STYLES.md), which records actual palette/layout support, and [MOTION-CHOICES.md](MOTION-CHOICES.md), which records scene contracts. They do not add families, data keys, transitions or palette controls to the runtime. Read [EDITORIAL.md](EDITORIAL.md) first; use [STYLE-LEARNING.md](STYLE-LEARNING.md) when named references inform the direction and [PROJECT-SCENES.md](PROJECT-SCENES.md) for a mechanism beyond a shipped family.

Across all directions, identify the phrase's function, choose one dominant action, retain the relevant object's identity and give its resolved state time to be understood. Use source-word cues for recordings and authored reading cues for graphics. Adapt frame counts, density and presenter use to the material. A motion recipe is a decision system, not a quota of effects or seconds.

## split-canvas — demonstrate while the speaker explains

- **Decision:** Choose it when seeing the speaker and a demonstration together builds understanding. A personal line returns to `presenter`; a detailed diagram or result can use `takeover` if the split would make it small.
- **Composition/material:** Give the upper product canvas one dominant card or relationship. Keep rounded UI surfaces, sans type and a small accent rule; subordinate supporting text. The presenter window and caption pill must remain distinct and clear. Use the supported profile palette rules in STYLES.
- **Timing/action:** Cue the meaningful UI state or diagram change on the reviewed phrase. Land it, demonstrate its cause, then hold the result. Keep the same UI/object through related steps rather than restarting with a title on every line.
- **Transition/sound:** Match the object's position across beats or use a clear state change. A click marks a real selected control; a soft landing can support a card arrival. Protect speech and give a personal line room.
- **Application/review:** Use `process`, `proof`, `screen-focus` or another validated split family when they fit. Check that the viewer can read the demo without a zoom that softens source text. If the upper canvas only repeats the subtitle, give it an explanatory job.

## section-deck — make the chapter structure visible

- **Decision:** Choose it when the argument naturally divides into sections, stages or comparison criteria. Define each section's question and answer before deciding how many scenes it needs.
- **Composition/material:** Use a dark section ground, a concise kicker, an accent headline and semantic chips. Keep the main answer visually stronger than the section label. Palette cycling follows the supported deck rules, not a different color for each sentence.
- **Timing/action:** Establish a section, reveal the relationship that explains it, and hold the answer. Stagger items in reading or causal order. A short spoken aside may keep the existing section without a new reveal.
- **Transition/sound:** Carry the section's selected item or direction into the next state. A chapter change can reset the ground after the prior thought resolves. Use restrained ticks for real stage changes; avoid a sting for every chip.
- **Application/review:** `process`, `comparison` and the appropriate semantic families can structure the material; check their actual layout support. Review whether the headings explain a progression rather than creating unrelated slide cards.

## kinetic-paper — make one statement land

- **Decision:** Choose it for a strong claim, question or contrast that benefits from poster-scale hierarchy. Break the argument into meaningful units rather than animating every spoken word equally.
- **Composition/material:** Let heavy poster type dominate, with ink frames or signal strips supporting a hierarchy. Reserve empty space around the important phrase. Keep mixed Arabic/English labels legible and joined; the style's own palette applies.
- **Timing/action:** Reveal subordinate context before the key statement, then let the statement land and hold. Use stagger to create a reading order, with movement stopped during the essential read.
- **Transition/sound:** Continue an ink edge, strip or key word into the next composition, or make a deliberate clean change after resolution. A single impact can mark the main claim; quieter supporting lines let that contrast work.
- **Application/review:** Use reviewed titles or a suitable `motion-concept`; literal `kinetic-text` requires real recorded word indices. Check that text motion expresses emphasis and leaves enough reading time, without treating authored titles as subtitles.

## calligraphic-receipts — connect the claim to its proof

- **Decision:** Choose it for evidence-led stories, claims and measured results. If evidence is missing, show a clearly labelled illustration or resolve the gap instead of inventing a receipt.
- **Composition/material:** Pair a Naskh claim with a receipt sheet, brush rule and quiet boxless captions. Let the source/value relationship dominate; a decorative stamp should never imply authenticity.
- **Timing/action:** State the claim, bring in the relevant evidence, reveal the decisive line or number, then hold the source and qualification together. Keep the evidence readable for its actual density.
- **Transition/sound:** Maintain the claim/receipt pairing while the evidence changes. A gentle paper placement or underline cue can locate the proof; silence can give a consequential number weight.
- **Application/review:** `proof`, `meter-receipt` or supplied evidence assets may fit, subject to their contracts. Confirm units, denominators, attribution, date/conditions and source legibility. A stylish receipt is not proof of a factual result.

## paper-collage — show how the pieces connect

- **Decision:** Choose it for an assembly, contrast or story whose pieces become a coherent whole. Every piece needs an explanatory role: input, evidence, alternative or result.
- **Composition/material:** Use taped-paper cards, tabs and restrained depth. Make the focal layer clear through scale, overlap and contrast; keep text on readable surfaces. Paper texture belongs to graphics and does not alter the presenter's likeness.
- **Timing/action:** Introduce pieces in meaning order, move the same pieces into their relationship and hold the assembled result. A selected piece can become the next scene's focus without losing its identity.
- **Transition/sound:** Match a card's edge, location or direction between beats. Paper moves and taps belong on the visible action; avoid constant rustling behind speech.
- **Application/review:** `message-board`, suitable concepts or a project scene can preserve individual pieces. Check the incoming/assembled/focused states for unwanted swaps, collisions and hidden labels. Adding more scraps is not a substitute for showing the relationship.

## judgment-board — compare on fair criteria

- **Decision:** Choose it when the viewer needs to weigh alternatives and understand a verdict. Establish the common criterion and available evidence before choosing a winner.
- **Composition/material:** Use a clear two-column board, divider and serif verdict hierarchy. Keep both sides comparably readable; make the criterion visible. The style's own palette and boxless captions apply.
- **Timing/action:** Establish the shared subject, reveal each side under the same criterion, then move attention to the decisive difference and hold the qualified verdict. Do not hide a weak point by revealing its side too briefly.
- **Transition/sound:** Keep the two sides stable while criteria or states change. A restrained rule/marker can locate a difference; give the final decision a quieter readable hold.
- **Application/review:** `comparison` or an authored board is suitable when actual fields fit. Verify that values share units and context. Equal layout is not evidence that unsupported claims are true; label opinion and uncertainty honestly.

## stepped-editorial — develop a thesis through details

- **Decision:** Choose it for an opinion, case study or story that starts with a thesis and advances through supporting details. Each new detail should change what the viewer understands.
- **Composition/material:** Build an editorial spread with offset plates, serif hierarchy and yellow-marker evidence. Treat the marked excerpt as a focal detail, not a highlighter applied to every line.
- **Timing/action:** Set the thesis, bring in an evidence/detail plate, guide attention to the relevant fragment, then resolve its implication. Hold the marked text before shifting the spread's hierarchy.
- **Transition/sound:** Carry the selected excerpt, plate edge or marker path into the next idea. A page/plate movement can connect paragraphs; a constant camera drift can reduce reading clarity.
- **Application/review:** Combine supported proof/comparison/process scenes or a project spread. Check the reading order and whether the evidence actually supports the thesis. Avoid several simultaneous plates competing with the source captions.

## liquid-glass — inspect a selected part of a system

- **Decision:** Choose it when a grouping, measurement or focused detail benefits from isolation. Identify what the lens reveals and how that detail belongs to the whole.
- **Composition/material:** Use a dark aurora, translucent panels and a bright rim. Keep enough opaque/contrasting support for text; glass is a graphic material, not a filter over the presenter or factual screen detail.
- **Timing/action:** Establish the whole, select a group or value, magnify/emphasize the meaningful part and hold the resulting explanation. Keep selection and the wider context visible where possible.
- **Transition/sound:** Continue the selected object through the focus move. A soft selection/settling cue can help; avoid adding a shiny sweep with no state change.
- **Application/review:** `token-lens` supports labelled grouping; `meter-receipt` supports two values on one positive maximum. Illustrative groups are not real tokenizer output unless supplied evidence establishes that. Review contrast, lens targets, units and glow that may obscure type.

## campaign-tickets — explain the invitation and its conditions

- **Decision:** Choose it for a real offer, invitation or viewer question. Establish the action the viewer can take and the terms they need before designing urgency.
- **Composition/material:** Use warm tickets, coral stubs and perforated surfaces. Let the offer/action dominate while the conditions remain readable; separate real attributed questions from illustrative examples.
- **Timing/action:** Reveal what is offered, demonstrate the concrete benefit, show conditions, then give the approved action time. A question stack reveals one meaningful question at a time, not an unreadable pile.
- **Transition/sound:** Carry a ticket or stub from invitation to action. A tear/click can accompany that graphic movement; it does not imply a purchase or completed transaction.
- **Application/review:** `ticket-offer` and `comment-stack` supply validated fields. Verify price/value, dates, conditions, attribution and exact approved CTA. Never add a fake countdown, scarcity, discount or testimonial to make the ticket look convincing.

## magazine-interview — preserve the speaker's authority and wording

- **Decision:** Choose it for a quote, interview chapter or attributed viewpoint. Identify whose words are being shown and which context makes them accurate.
- **Composition/material:** Use a navy editorial page, gold rules, clear quote text and speaker identification. Keep the chapter marker secondary. An optional supplied transparent cover subject uses the exact asset contract in STYLES.
- **Timing/action:** Establish the chapter/speaker, reveal the exact quote at its real cue or authored reading moment, then hold attribution and words together. Give the voice room when the face carries the statement.
- **Transition/sound:** Use a restrained chapter/rule change or a deliberate portrait-to-quote relationship. Avoid effects that obscure a speaker's face or imply an unspoken emphasis.
- **Application/review:** `chapter-quote` carries title, quote, speaker and role; check all against the source. Review cover overlap and the quote transition if enabled. A shorter paraphrase belongs in a reviewed explanatory label, not quotation marks.

## scrapbook-route — carry a journey through connected stops

- **Decision:** Choose it for a journey, sequence or milestone story where the relationship between stops matters. Distinguish chronology/process from geographic location.
- **Composition/material:** Use a warm paper route, grid marks, taped photos and station markers. Keep a stable route and visibly selected stop; photo detail supports that stop rather than replacing its label.
- **Timing/action:** Establish the route, advance to each stop, reveal its meaning, then hold the combined journey. Retain visited stops when their context matters to the final explanation.
- **Transition/sound:** Move along the actual connected path and let the next marker settle. A soft travel/tap cue can mark arrival; avoid teleporting a marker across disconnected scenes.
- **Application/review:** `route-stops` supports two to four stops and optional verified local images; more complex movement can use a project scene. A diagram is not a factual map: verified borders/locations need supplied geometry. Check path endpoints, names, photo provenance and final context.

## Select, adapt and prove the direction

Recommend a direction by the message's dominant function and source needs, preserving an explicit or saved fixed choice. Compare the same representative passage when alternatives remain consequential. Implement the relevant decisions in actual scene data/code and asset placement, then review through [VISUAL-REVIEW.md](VISUAL-REVIEW.md). A matching palette alone does not show style fidelity, and a successful technical gate does not establish creative acceptance.
